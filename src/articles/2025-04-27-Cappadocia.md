What I need is a week of warm weather training before the JK I thought. Wow there is a four day event in Cappadocia in Turkey I thought. Cappadocia is known for two things: the scenery dominated by amazing rock formations, and the balloon flights over the scenery. What’s not to like I thought.

<!--more-->

The town of Goreme in Cappadocia, central Turkey, turns out to be at 1200m above sea level, so just a bit higher than Snowdon. I was there for seven days and it snowed on five of them. The temperature hovered just above freezing most days albeit with quite a lot of sunshine and blue skies. The first rule of warm weather training turns out to be to check that the weather will be warm. But I had a fantastic week anyway.

The event consisted of three days of training followed by four races. Just under 300 people entered with about half being locals and the rest drawn mainly from around Europe with large numbers of Swedes, Norwegians, Finns and Swiss. Four other Brits made the trip, with Karen, Grace and Alice French from WIM competing whilst Andy French settled for a tourist week.

Getting there was relatively simple but did involve the penultimate flight out of Heathrow at 22.35 on a Monday evening. This meant arriving in the new and enormous Istanbul airport at about 4.00 local time and having to wait four hours for the connecting flight so I didn’t get a great deal of sleep. I was awake enough to get the correct flight to Nevsehir rather than the next flight on the departure board which was to Batman, a town near the Syrian border it turns out. A local bus then did a tour of hotels in various towns and finally dropped me at the Royal Stone Houses in Goreme, complete with a private balcony overlooking what turned out to be the Day 3 area.

The next day was the final training day. I woke at about 6.00 and looked out of the window to find the sky full of low cloud and balloons. Quickly getting dressed I walked up the hill behind the hotel and took in the full madness of what was going on. I counted at least 70 ballons visible in the sky all around, and would estimate that there were probably around 150 in total. They were taking off from across the valley to the east and landing on the ridges above me to the west. The baskets were enormous, with the small ones taking 20 people and the big ones 30 people. Each balloon requires a truck and trailer, plus associated minibuses or open-top red, yellow, pink or blue cadillacs to move the passengers. Everywhere you went on the ridge above the hotel there were ballons landing directly onto the trailers before the passengers got out to have breakfast. The logistics of all this were staggering. Flights wee being quoted at about £100 per person, so with 150 balloons averaging 20 people that means there was over a quarter of a million pounds flying overhead. It turned out that the weather for the week was not great so this only happened on two of the days I was there, but in Summer it is every day.

The training itself was in Goreme. I arrived just in time for it to start raining. The area was covered in crags and rock pillars and you soon worked out that the critical thing was to find ways through the larger crags, as well as avoiding the queues of older Scandinavians trying to get up the steep slopes.
Day 1 was a 20 minute bus journey to Mustafapasa. This included the mandatory diversion when the bus had to turn around to avoid an accident in the snow on the bypass. The race started and ended in the town and took in a series of rocky valleys around the town complete with a scattering of snow on the ground. I just spotted the critical route choice in time on the way down through the crags to control 4 and despite losing a minute at the end in the town it turned out that I had won the day by three minutes.

Day 2 was a sprint in the old town of Avanos. I decided to get there the scenic way with an eight kilometer walk from the hotel down through a rocky valley and then a series of large fields. This was a morning full of bird life, with large numbers of hoopoes, crested larks, corn buntings and tree sparrows. Hoopoes were probably the bird of the week, turning up everywhere and with their oop-oop-oop calls echoing around the rocks they could be heard from quite a distance. The race itself was classic European old town with multiple twisty lanes and passageways set on a slope above the river. I took a couple of straight routes that would have been better round but still managed to win by a minute.

Day 3 was in Goreme itself on the rocky slopes I could see from my hotel balcony. This was the most technical day staying in complex rock pillar areas for most of the course. An early route choice error taking the wrong way around an out of bounds area cost well over a minute, but I just about held it together and slowed down for the last two controls to avoid any late disasters. That turned out to be enough for a win by 37 seconds.

Day 4 was a sprint around the old town of Uchisar with its castle that had been visible on the skyline all week. My walk up Pigeon Valley to the event include over 400m of climb through more fresh snow and mud. The name comes because large numbers of holes were cut out of the rock to house pigeons as a source of fertilizer. The race started into a very steep valley below the castle where it was almost impossible to stand up on the gravelly slopes as you ran from rock pillar to rock pillar. After that there was a lot more climb around the twisty roads on the other side of the castle. 2.5km (with a measured 150m of climb) took me over 20 minutes but that was enough to be two minutes quicker than anybody else so I had my four race wins. Karen and Alice won overall as well, with Grace second, so it was a medal for each of the Brits.

Overall a fantastic week and I would thoroughly recommend a visit. I fitted in an awful lot of walking when not competing and the scenery was stunning. You can see full results plus maps and photos on the [event website](https://www.cappadociaoweek.com/index.php).
