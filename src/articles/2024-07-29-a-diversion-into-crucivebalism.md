Many readers (but, let’s not kid ourselves, mainly of the more elderly persuasion) will recognise the name Willie Rushton. He was an actor, cartoonist and comedian who co-founded “Private Eye”. His book "Pigsticking, A Joy for Life" provided brief details of how to participate in nearly every sport imaginable, and includes a section on orienteering.

<!--more-->

I quote:

> At international level it is the survival of the fittest, not only the fittest but an even rarer breed who can also read intelligently on the trot through unknown terrain. In the broader view, it is car-rallying for pedestrians. The Swedes invented it to alleviate the boring nature of running. I can only run 50 yards at best, and am bored stiff in that short time. If I was doing The Times crossword, however, at the same time, I would be arse over elbow in the first five feet, but interested and amused. That is orienteering. Armed with a map, a compass and a list of control points, the competitors set off at one-minute intervals into the unknown, and unknown it is. Not only is the course a total secret until the start, but its very location also is until as late as possible. It is quite often composed under the guise of a NATO operation or such. So bang goes the obvious device of having your family, the more elderly, the children, line some part of the fast lane, to wave Swedish flags, shout encouragement and point vigorously in the wrong direction. Better that they enter. It is an ideal occupation for both tortoise and hare.
>
> To this end, competitors are offered a choice of routes, the classic alternative is "over or round", and while the young and virile can pick a swift belt down a sheep-track for a mile, some of us may well prefer to make our own way less actively to the same objective through a couple of hundred yards of light jungle. The speediest mind wins. The experts consult their maps every two seconds while moving at pace. One move in the wrong direction and you're doomed. The forests echo with the distant cries of failed orienteers.

And so with the link between orienteering and crossword firmly established I can move on the Listener Crossword. Wikipedia has this down as “ generally regarded as the most difficult cryptic crossword to appear in a national weekly” so who am I to argue. A typical Listener will have a hidden theme (albatrosses, the works of Ed Sheeran and Adele, the second names of US Presidents to list just three recent examples) and a preamble that presents a fiendishly complicated set of instructions to be followed. Compilers are simply identified by pseudonyms.

It started off in _“The Listener”_ but now appears in _"The Times"_ on Saturdays. For addicts it is published on their website on a Friday evening. I normally download it on a Friday and would hope to be getting close to finishing, or at least understand what is going on, by the end of the weekend. I manage to finish most of them eventually, although it can take quite a long time. At WOC 2024 I was Media Operation Manager and was extremely busy pretty much non-stop. I certainly didn’t download it on the Friday, never got around to buying the Times on the Saturday and therefore the first time I saw **The Listener Crossword No 4824 There and Back Again by Lysander** was on Wednesday evening when I got home from Edinburgh. Here is the preamble I was faced with.

> Every clue has a letter misprinted in the definition. In clue order the correct letters yield a series of rules, each consisting of a 1ac and a 10. The 1ac is one or two letters from a group of four. That is followed by one of the other 22 letters of the alphabet, and the last digit of its alphabetical position (A/K/U = 1, B/L/V = 2 etc) is the 10. Starting in the bottom right cell, solvers must draw a series of straight lines joining centres of cells, guided by the rules (never visiting any cell more than once), plus a final line back to the start, creating a closed path. The letters in the “turning” cells spell a word; that and the other four unclued entries together identify an event and its location.

The four unclued words were 5, 6, 9 and 13 letters. In the broad spread of Listener complications I’d rate this as about TD 3 to 4 making it a hard Orange or Light Green course. After my standard quick read through of all the clues to spot anything obvious I had managed to fill in two or three. Some more detailed thinking got another three or four and it was time to see if anything could be guessed. The unclued 13 letter word had an H as the second letter and a P as the penultimate letter. Chairmanships, chimney-sweeps and chromotograph all looked hopeful but then I spotted championships. This was definitely correct since Sunday had been the Euros finals and this was going to be a Euros-based theme. Five minutes later it was clear that this definitely wasn’t correct and I was left wondering what other championships this could be. Assuming the C of championships was correct allowed me to solve 1 down and I was left with 1 across starting BE. That could be BEARING, and 10 down would then be DISTANCE (fitting with the A in championships) and that was pretty much it.

I claim that I got the four unclued words (WORLD, SPRINT, CHAMPIONSHIPS, EDINBURGH) faster, and with fewer known letters, than any other solver, and will have been one of the very few who managed to do this without needing to Google to find out what World Championships were happening in Edinburgh that week. The “turning” cells ended up spelling out ORIENTEERING. There will of course be other orienteers who are regular solvers (since that is what orienteers are like) but the obvious question was whether it had been an orienteer who set the puzzle.

There is a [Listener Crossword website](https://www.listenercrossword.com/List_Home.html) (of course there is) which includes a full list of every crossword published along with who compiled it (of course it does). And so I was quickly able to determine that Lysander was Anthony Covey-Crump, aided and abetted by Aedites who turned out to be Dr P A K Covey-Crump.

Many readers (not necessarily so old this time, but needing to have been in HH between about 2004 and 2010) will recognise that Tony was an HH member when he was not running for SLOW or JOK. Congratulations to Tony on getting this published.
