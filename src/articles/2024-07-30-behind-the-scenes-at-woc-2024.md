Many of you will have had a chance to watch some of the racing at this year’s World Orienteering Championships in Edinburgh, either in person or via the various TV broadcasts of the event. If you haven’t yet seen anything then you can still catch up on the [Eurovision Sport website](https://eurovisionsport.com/en). This was the second sprint-only WOC, after Denmark 2022, and over the course of five days there were three races: individual sprint, mixed sprint relay and Knock-out sprint.

<!--more-->

WOC 2024 was originally meant to be WOC 2022 but got moved back when Covid arrived. That meant that many of the people involved had been working on the event for five years. In Denmark in 2022 I had volunteered to Jon Cross, the Assistant Event Director, that I would do something useful. He managed to fill various other key roles and by January 2024 was left needing to appoint a “Media Operations Manager”. I had previously registered as “Media” at WOCs in Estonia and Latvia so I was by far the most qualified (and only) candidate available. Despite neither of us really knowing what this would involve I joined the central management team (referred to in meetings as LOT, although I still have no idea what that stood for) and started doing whatever felt useful to make sure that “Media” was taken care of for WOC 2024.

The first step was to sign a non-disclosure agreement to confirm that I wouldn’t pass on any information to those who might be taking part or anyone else likely to be involved in the event. This was a continued theme throughout everything that happened. Maps, courses, exact arena locations, arena layouts and even how far the start was from the finish were kept secret for as long as possible. Access to the WOC 2024 Dropbox for file sharing was split so that only certain people could see certain directories. In meetings you needed to be careful since not everybody had signed the NDA so you couldn’t always discuss everything you knew. If you check the various bulletins you will find that we never published any arena diagrams for example. This made life somewhat complicated when I was trying to brief photographers about where they could go but had nothing that showed the layout.

We held hour-long on-line LOT meetings every two weeks to keep everybody up to date and work out what needed doing next. These normally had about 10 to 15 attendees covering courses and planning, arenas, WOC Office, WOC Tour, IT (everything to do with event timing and results), commercial, finance and of course Media and TV. There were then extra on-line meetings as necessary to consider specific aspects such as arena design (through several iterations), IT requirements and media arrangements. I also went up to Scotland to help at Euromeeting in Stirling in October 2023 and Sprint Scotland in Glenrothes in May 2024. Both of these had high-class international fields as runners got used to Scottish sprint terrain, and also allowed us to test the technology in action, including novelties such as photo finishes.

The three days after Sprint Scotland saw the final site visit by the IOF team that were overseeing the event. This was a really useful chance to visit all five race areas, see exactly what space was available and how things all fitted together, agree on radio and TV control locations as well as get to meet various people that I had only ever seen on-line before.

The IOF publish quite a lot of detailed guidance about how to stage a WOC. This includes coverage of Media, TV and Social Media. Everything to do with TV for WOC 2024 was handled by former GBR WOC runner Lorna Eades, so I could refer any question to her. Social media was managed via a contract with Flip, an agency that specializes in whatever social media is. They had HH runner Sam Fielding in their team to provide inside orienteering knowledge. That means I was just left with the “media” bit.

The first main issue was to sort out accreditation. All media were meant to register through the IOF on-line Eventor system. We ended up with 18 photographers (split into six P1 who get access to the best locations and 12 P2), 15 journalists and 16 TV, split into right holders and non-rights holders. Finalising this list took quite a lot of effort and discussion with Erling Thisted, the IOF Communications Officer who was my contact at the IOF. Applications for a four-person TV crew from Iran and a photographer from Pakistan were politely declined. Various photographers got moved between P1 and P2, and then there were the inevitable complications such as how the US Team could pay for a journalist’s bus pass, or how a person already registered as an IOF VIP could register as a journalist as well. Once accreditation was sorted I emailed everybody to confirm they had been accepted and to send out a copy of the Media Bulletin that I had written.

Peter and I travelled up to Edinburgh by train on Wednesday 10th July and headed to Pollock Halls which was the WOC Event Centre. Many of the WOC teams were staying here, as well as all of the key organising team. There were three WOC race days, on Friday, Sunday and Tuesday, and a routine soon emerged.

The day before was spent finalising and issuing a Media Newsletter with the latest details about the arrangements for the next day such as Media Centre locations and travel details. I had a log-in to the WOC 2024 website so I could make my own updates to that and add links to photos and newsletters. In the afternoon I attended the Commentators’ Briefing. This was led by Katherine Bett of SN who was doing the English commentary for the IOF TV. The briefings provided an opportunity for the planners to present the courses and explain the overall course philosophy and to highlight the critical legs and expected times for various route choices. There are some fascinating details about how the courses evolved, what access restrictions had to be dealt with and how the courses were influenced by TV requirements. Hopefully these will be publicised more widely in the near future.

After that there was an opportunity to visit the next day’s arena, or at least as much of it as they had managed to build by that stage. This allowed final adjustments to be discussed to who could go where behind the scenes. And finally back at Pollock Halls there was a late evening Management Team meeting to make sure everything was ready and to sort out any last-minute problems.

For two of the days there was an early-morning trip out to the Qualification race where we had very limited media facilities but there were still lots of people around asking for maps and needing to be kept in order. After that it was a rush back to the main race arena and a day spent making sure the Media Centre was set up and that people had everything they needed. Desks needed to be arranged and provided with numerous gang sockets. Wi-fi details had to be provided, big screens set up to show live TV coverage, start lists printed and handed out (which on the first day involved me carrying a laser printer 500m to the Media Centre and then 500m back to the arena since we had no vehicle access between where it was and where it needed to be) and most importantly of all the maps needed to be distributed. This involved secretive meet-ups with Paul Murgatroyd, the man with all the maps, who would turn up with sealed envelopes to be handed over but only after it was confirmed that all athletes were safely locked up in Quarantine.

I gave a media briefing about an hour before each race, and there was then a guided tour of the arena to point out where people were meant and not meant to go. On day one we also handed out IOF Photo vests to all photographers. I tended to spend my time in the Media Centre watching everything on TV, leaving others to manage everything at the arena. I had asked Alan and Janet Rosen to run the Mixed Zone, which is where TV interviews take place immediately after a runner has finished. Nobody was quite sure exactly what this involved, but by the end of the week Alan and Janet seemed to have worked things out as they negotiated with TV crews and athletes to decide who would go where and when.

After each race had finished there was then a frantic period in the Media Centre as people uploaded their photographs and race reports whilst we tried to tidy up and encourage people to leave before we got thrown out.

In amongst all that James and Peter managed to run all the WOC Tour races. I only fitted in two, since there wasn’t time to run and then get to the WOC Final on two of the days, and because I was being somewhat sensible after making a large hole in my shin that required a hospital visit at the HH Northaw event the week before WOC. We also got roped in to help at the traditional IOF VIP/Media Race which had somehow taken a back seat to the other races that week. James ended up hanging controls and Peter guarded a control. All of the media details are still on the [WOC 2024 website](https://www.woc2024.org/index.html) if you want to find out even more about what went on over the week.

With thanks to my team of media volunteers that did whatever was needed whenever I asked them: John Birtwistle , Sue Birtwistle, Sarah Brown, James Errington, Peter Errington, Penny Parkes, Alan Rosen, Janet Rosen and Paul Street.
